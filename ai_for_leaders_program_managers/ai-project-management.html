What we just learned

This whole module lives inside Step 1 of the lifecycle: problem scoping and planning. Project management in AI rests on three pillars — planning, coordination, and business alignment — and the most common failure mode isn't a bad model, it's a team that shipped a working model nobody asked the right question about.

🎯 CTO view

"We have data and a model" is the sentence that precedes most AI project write-offs. Require a named business KPI before funding moves past step 1 — not after the model exists.

📋 PM view

Because new models ship weekly, a plan that assumed today's tooling is stale within a quarter. Build re-evaluation checkpoints into the plan itself, not just the backlog.

02InceptionV8M2Q41

Vision, Feasibility & Scope

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Before checking whether an AI project is technically possible, what should you check first?
  2. What's the actual risk of skipping a scope definition and just "seeing where the model takes us"?

Defining your North Star

Three questions anchor the vision before anything else gets planned:

1

Why this project?

  • Quantify expected business value
  • 15% cost reduction?
  • $1M annual revenue growth?
  • 3,000 labor hours saved?
2

Alignment with strategic goals

  • Customer retention improvement?
  • Operational efficiency?
  • New revenue streams?
3

Clear success metrics

  • Well-defined KPIs
  • Track ROI
  • Measure what matters

Feasibility assessment — 4 dimensions

DimensionKey questions
Technical feasibilityRight tools/infrastructure? Cloud (AWS/GCP/Azure) available? Can we prototype quickly? Is the model technically achievable for this use case?
Organizational readinessSkilled data scientists, ML engineers, AI PMs on hand? Can we hire or consult if needed?
Data availability & qualityAccess to the right data? Historical/real-time labeled data? Is it clean? How long to prepare?
Cost & resource constraintsDeliverable within budget and timeline? Can AutoML or pre-trained open-source models cut cost?

Scope definition — MVP first, avoid scope creep

Practical example: a self-driving car project.

✅ In scope (MVP)
  • Highway driving only
  • Lane keeping on highways
  • Adaptive cruise control
  • Automatic lane changes in clear conditions
  • Clear daylight + good weather
❌ Out of scope (future phases)
  • Urban driving — traffic lights, pedestrians
  • Complex lighting & weather — snow, rain, fog
  • Construction zones
  • Night-time driving
What we just learned

Vision comes before feasibility, and feasibility comes before scope. You quantify why the project matters and how it ties to strategy first; only then do you check whether it's technically, organizationally, and financially achievable; only then do you draw a hard MVP line — what ships now versus what's explicitly deferred — because an undefined scope is how "highway lane-keeping" quietly turns into "full self-driving everywhere" and the project never ships.

🎯 CTO view

Feasibility is a go/no-go gate, not a formality — a project that's organizationally infeasible (no ML engineer on staff) needs a hiring or consulting line item before it's greenlit, not a hopeful assumption.

🏗️ Architect view

Design the MVP's architecture to be extensible toward the out-of-scope items, even though you're not building them yet — retrofitting night-time or weather handling onto a highway-only architecture is far more expensive than anticipating the seam now.

📋 PM view

Write the out-of-scope list down and get stakeholder sign-off on it explicitly — an unwritten "we'll get to that later" is exactly where scope creep enters through the back door.

03MeasurementT5R9L03

Success Metrics — 3 Levels

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Can a model with excellent accuracy still be a business failure? What would that look like?
  2. Where would "response time" belong — model metrics, system metrics, or business metrics?
FIG 3.1 — THREE LEVELS OF MEASUREMENTeach level answers a different question
LevelMeasuresExamples
Model qualityWhat the model producesClassification: accuracy, precision, recall, F1. LLMs: coherence, instruction adherence.
System qualityHow well it runs in productionLatency, throughput, uptime, resource efficiency (CPU/GPU usage)
BusinessReal-world impactProductivity gains, cost savings, customer experience, revenue growth
What we just learned

A model can be correct (model metrics), fast and reliable (system metrics), and still miss the point if it doesn't move a business number (business metrics). All three levels have to clear the bar together — optimizing one in isolation, like chasing accuracy at the cost of latency, can quietly sink the other two.

🎯 CTO view

Report all three levels to leadership, not just model accuracy — a board that only hears "94% accuracy" has no way to judge whether the project is actually working in production or moving the business.

📋 PM view

Assign an owner to each level — data science owns model metrics, engineering owns system metrics, the business sponsor owns business metrics — so no level quietly goes unmeasured.

04Goal SettingS2Q6D87

SMART Goals Framework

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. What's wrong with the goal "improve the chatbot's response time"? What's it missing?
LetterMeansExample
SSpecificReduce customer service response time using an AI chatbot
MMeasurable40% reduction, from 5 min to 3 min
AAchievableBased on industry benchmarks and current capacity
RRelevantAligns with improving UX and reducing cost
TTime-boundWithin 3 months, maintaining CSAT > 85%
Complete SMART goal

"Reduce average customer service response time by 40% (from 5 minutes to 3 minutes) by deploying an AI-powered chatbot within 3 months, while maintaining customer satisfaction score above 85%."

What we just learned

A SMART goal isn't five separate checkboxes — it's one sentence that survives all five tests at once. "Improve response time" fails on measurable, achievable, and time-bound; the complete example bundles a number, a deadline, and a guardrail metric (CSAT) so success can't be redefined after the fact.

📋 PM view

The guardrail metric (CSAT > 85%) is the part teams skip under deadline pressure — without it, a team can "hit" the speed goal by giving worse answers faster. Always pair the primary SMART metric with at least one guardrail.

05PeopleP9H4W15

Stakeholders & Roles

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Which role bridges business and technical teams — and what happens to a project without one?
  2. What's the difference between a Data Scientist and a Machine Learning Engineer, in one sentence?

Category 1 — Business & Product

01

AI Product Manager

Bridges technical & business teams, defines vision & roadmap, translates business problems into ML objectives, sets success metrics. AI initiatives fail without this role.

02

Industry Domain Expert

Provides domain knowledge, validates data relevance, guides feature engineering, explains outputs in business context. Without this, models can mislead or even harm.

03

Project Manager

Coordinates all moving parts, tracks milestones & deliverables, manages risk and budget, facilitates cross-functional communication.

Category 2 — Data Science & AI

01

AI Researcher

Often PhD-level. Develops novel algorithms, publishes research, pushes the boundaries of AI theory. (e.g. "Attention Is All You Need.")

02

Data Scientist

Analyzes data and builds models, uses statistical analysis, performs feature engineering, communicates insights. Turns raw data into business value.

03

ML Engineer

Builds scalable ML pipelines, deploys to production, tunes for performance and scalability. Takes models from notebook to production.

04

Responsible AI & Ethics Lead

Audits for bias and discrimination, ensures fairness and explainability, enforces ethical practice. Prevents reputational damage.

05

UX Designer

Designs intuitive interfaces, creates user journeys for AI outputs, builds trust and usability. If users can't interact with it, the AI is useless.

Category 3 — Development & Ops

01

Software Engineer

Builds applications and APIs, connects AI models to user interfaces, ensures code quality and scalability. The best model is useless without integration.

02

Solution Architect

Designs the technical blueprint, defines system architecture, chooses tools and platforms, ensures scalability and security. Poor architecture means a slow, expensive system.

03

MLOps Architect

Automates deployment (CI/CD), monitors model performance, manages versioning and rollback, detects model drift. AI must run reliably in production.

Category 4 — Data Engineering

01

Data Engineer

Builds ETL pipelines, cleans and structures raw data, integrates data sources. No data means no AI — data is the fuel.

02

Data Architect

Designs data infrastructure, defines data models and schema, governs access and retention. Without sound architecture, data becomes siloed, slow, or expensive.

What we just learned

An AI project needs four distinct clusters of people — business/product, data science/AI, development/ops, and data engineering — and each has a role whose absence has a named, predictable failure mode: no AI PM means misaligned scope, no domain expert means misleading models, no MLOps means unreliable production, no data engineer means no fuel at all.

🎯 CTO view

Headcount planning should map to these four categories explicitly — a team of five data scientists and zero data engineers or MLOps staff is not a smaller version of a full team, it's a team that structurally cannot reach production.

📋 PM view

When a role is missing (common in small teams — see the UnitedHealth case later), don't silently absorb its responsibilities into an adjacent role. Name the gap and either hire, consult, or explicitly de-scope what that missing role would have covered.

06MethodologyW7N3B68

Waterfall vs Agile

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Why would a methodology built for construction (Waterfall) struggle specifically with AI projects?
  2. What does "iterative and incremental" actually buy you that "linear and sequential" doesn't?
FIG 6.1 — WATERFALL: LINEAR & SEQUENTIALtesting happens last
FIG 6.2 — AGILE: ITERATIVE & INCREMENTALworking software every sprint
AspectWaterfallAgile
ApproachLinear & sequentialIterative & incremental
FlexibilityInflexible — changes are hardHighly flexible — changes accommodated
PhasesDistinct, completed onceRepeated each sprint
Customer involvementLimited — mostly start/endContinuous, regular feedback
TestingAfter development completesOngoing throughout
DeliverySingle delivery at the endWorking product in increments
Risk managementHigher — issues surface lateLower — frequent reassessment
Team collaborationTop-down, siloedCross-functional, daily stand-ups
Best forSimple, well-defined (construction)Complex, evolving (AI, startups)
ExamplesBridges, manufacturing, regulated buildsSoftware startups, AI/ML, mobile/web
What we just learned

Waterfall assumes you can fully specify the outcome up front — true for a bridge, false for a model whose accuracy you can't know until you've trained it. Agile's core advantage for AI isn't speed, it's that testing and stakeholder feedback happen continuously instead of at the very end, so a bad direction gets caught in week 2 instead of month 6. AI projects are exploratory by nature — Agile is almost always the better default.

🎯 CTO view

If a regulated business unit insists on Waterfall-style sign-offs, don't fight it head-on — layer Agile sprints inside Waterfall-style milestones (see the Hybrid framework next) rather than picking one pure methodology.

🏗️ Architect view

Non-determinism is the real argument against Waterfall for AI — a model may never reach a "finished" state the way a bridge does. Architecture needs to support continuous redeployment, not a single go-live event.

07FrameworksF4C8Y29

CRISP-DM, Agile & Hybrid Frameworks

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. CRISP-DM comes from data mining, Agile comes from software. What problem does combining them into "Hybrid" actually solve?
  2. Which framework would you guess is most common in regulated industries like banking — and why?
FIG 7.1 — CRISP-DM: SIX STRUCTURED PHASEScross-industry standard process for data mining

CRISP-DM: ✅ structured, phase-based, heavy upfront planning. ❌ slow feedback loops, stakeholders only at key milestones. Best for fixed-scope projects.

FIG 7.2 — AGILE: SUCCESSIVE SPRINTSplan → design → develop → test → review, repeated

Agile: ✅ fast feedback, frequent collaboration (daily stand-ups), rapid prototyping. ❌ can lack documentation. Best for research, startups, evolving projects.

FIG 7.3 — HYBRID: CRISP-DM PHASES + AGILE SPRINTS INSIDEgovernance and adaptability together

Hybrid: ✅ best of both — governance + adaptability, structured milestones + Agile flexibility, medium-fast feedback. Best for enterprise AI and regulated industries.

AspectCRISP-DMAgileHybrid
OriginData science methodologySoftware developmentBlend of both
StructureLinear & phase-basedIterative & cyclicalStructured milestones + Agile sprints
FocusComprehensive project phasesRapid experimentation & deliveryAligning business goals with execution
PlanningHeavy upfrontLight + sprintsInitial roadmap + flexible sprints
Team interactionKey milestonesDaily stand-upsSprint reviews + milestone check-ins
Feedback speedSlowFastMedium-fast
Best forStructured, fixed-scopeRapid prototyping & tuningEnterprise AI, regulated industries
RiskMisalignment with real-time changesLack of structure/documentationCombines governance & adaptability
What we just learned

CRISP-DM gives structure but slow feedback; Agile gives speed but weak documentation; Hybrid takes CRISP-DM's phase gates as the outer skeleton and runs Agile sprints inside each phase — which is why it dominates in regulated, enterprise settings that need both an audit trail and the ability to course-correct quickly.

🎯 CTO view

Choose the framework based on your compliance burden, not team preference — a bank's model risk management office will often mandate CRISP-DM-style phase gates regardless of how the engineering team wants to work; Hybrid is usually the pragmatic answer.

📋 PM view

In Hybrid, the milestone reviews are where you renegotiate scope with executives — treat them as real checkpoints, not rubber-stamp meetings, or you lose the one structural advantage Hybrid has over pure Agile.

08ScaleA1G5J74

SAFe — Scaled Agile Framework

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Agile works well for a single team. What specifically breaks when you have 10 Agile teams working on the same product?

SAFe is a structured methodology for scaling Agile, Lean, and DevOps practices across large enterprises.

FIG 8.1 — AGILE RELEASE TRAIN (ART)50–125 people, cross-functional
01

Alignment

All teams work toward common strategic goals, breaking down silos and forming the ART.

02

Faster time to market

Synchronized planning, regular delivery of value increments, quick customer feedback loops.

03

Increased productivity

Streamlined processes, improved team efficiency, transparency in work progress.

04

Continuous improvement

Improvement with every iteration, enhanced collaboration, better risk management.

Core values: Transparency · Alignment · Relentless Improvement · Respect for People

Companies using SAFe: Spotify, Intel, JP Morgan, and others operating multiple Agile teams at enterprise scale.

What we just learned

SAFe doesn't replace Agile — it coordinates many Agile teams (an ART of 50-125 people) around one shared backlog and delivery cadence, so ten teams moving independently don't drift into ten incompatible directions. It trades some of small-team Agile's speed for enterprise-scale alignment.

🎯 CTO view

SAFe is overhead you shouldn't adopt until you actually have the coordination problem it solves — a single 8-person AI team doesn't need an ART; introducing SAFe early just adds ceremony without the scale that justifies it.

Risks & Mitigation

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Of the five risk categories you're about to see, which do you think is most often underestimated at project kickoff?
1. Data risks
⚠ Identify
  • Poor quality data
  • Insufficient quantity
  • Inherent biases
  • Privacy concerns / PII exposure
🛡 Mitigate
  • Thorough data assessment early
  • Implement data governance
  • Ethical data sourcing
  • Data augmentation strategies
  • Anonymize personal information
2. Technical feasibility risks
⚠ Identify
  • Model may not achieve required accuracy
  • Very high computational cost
  • Complex integration challenges
🛡 Mitigate
  • Start with a Proof of Concept
  • Set realistic expectations
  • Explore multiple algorithms
  • Plan for scalability from day 1
3. Stakeholder alignment risks
⚠ Identify
  • Misunderstanding of the business problem
  • Changing expectations
  • Lack of buy-in from teams/leadership
🛡 Mitigate
  • Continuous communication
  • Involve stakeholders early and often
  • Clearly define success metrics
  • Ensure executive sponsorship
4. Operational & deployment risks
⚠ Identify
  • Latency issues
  • Scalability problems
  • Model drift post-deployment
  • Security vulnerabilities
🛡 Mitigate
  • Implement MLOps early
  • Design for production from day 1
  • Plan for monitoring & retraining
  • Regular security audits
5. Ethical & compliance risks
⚠ Identify
  • Lack of fairness / bias in the model
  • Lack of transparency
  • Regulatory non-compliance
🛡 Mitigate
  • Establish ethical guidelines
  • Conduct regular bias audits
  • Ensure explainability where needed
  • Consult legal & compliance teams
What we just learned

AI project risk isn't one bucket — it spans data, technical feasibility, stakeholder alignment, operations, and ethics/compliance, and each has its own mitigation playbook. Most of these mitigations share a pattern: do the hard, unglamorous work early (governance, PoCs, stakeholder involvement, MLOps, audits) rather than discovering the risk after launch.

🎯 CTO view

Stakeholder alignment risk is the one that doesn't show up in a technical risk register but sinks the most projects — track it with the same rigor (owner, mitigation plan, review cadence) you'd give a technical risk.

🏗️ Architect view

"Design for production from day 1" is the single highest-leverage line in this list — retrofitting monitoring, drift detection, and security onto a system built only for a notebook demo is far more expensive than building it in from the start.

What we just learned

Budgeting an AI project means pricing six categories, not just compute: infrastructure, people, tools, data, ongoing ops, and a hidden-costs buffer — a typical project lands between $15K and $450K+, dwarfed entirely by frontier-model training (~$100M) and its ongoing inference bill (~$700K/day). 🎯 CTO view

Budget inference as an operating expense line, not a footnote to the training capex — for any product with real usage, ongoing inference cost typically dwarfs the one-time training or fine-tuning cost within the first year.

📋 PM view

Reserve 10-15% of budget explicitly for the "hidden costs" category (legal, compliance, upskilling) — projects that don't plan for it usually eat that cost out of the operations budget instead, which then looks like a maintenance overrun.

Active RecallQ4X8N57

Test yourself — deep thought questions

If a model achieves 99% accuracy but takes 5 seconds to respond, is it better than a 95%-accurate model that responds in 100ms? How do you decide?
It depends entirely on the use case's latency budget and error tolerance: a fraud-review queue can tolerate 5 seconds because a human reviews the flag anyway, while a live chat or an autocomplete feature cannot — users will abandon it long before 5 seconds. The right way to decide is to set the system-level SLA (latency, uptime) as a hard constraint up front, then optimize model accuracy within that budget, rather than picking the "best" model on accuracy alone and hoping latency works out.
In the UnitedHealth case, the team has only 2 data analysts and 1 software engineer. Is the 6-month MVP feasible? What additional support is needed?
It's feasible specifically because the scope was deliberately narrowed to match the team's capacity — structured data only, AutoML instead of custom modeling, human-in-the-loop review instead of full automation — plus an AI consultant supplementing the missing roles (PM, data engineer, domain expert, ML engineer). Without that scope discipline and the consultant, the same team attempting a broader, custom-modeled, real-time system would not be feasible in 6 months.
CRISP-DM has structured phases, Agile has sprints, Hybrid combines both. Which would you recommend for a highly regulated industry like healthcare or finance, and why?
Hybrid is usually the right default for regulated industries: the CRISP-DM-style phase gates give compliance and risk teams the audit trail and formal sign-off points they require, while the Agile sprints nested inside each phase let the team course-correct on model performance and stakeholder feedback without waiting for the next full phase gate. Pure CRISP-DM is often too slow to adapt to data reality; pure Agile often lacks the documentation trail regulators expect.
If you have unlimited budget but limited talent, would you build or buy AI solutions? What are the trade-offs?
With genuinely limited talent, buying (pre-trained models, AutoML, vendor platforms) is usually the pragmatic choice regardless of budget, because the bottleneck isn't money — it's the people needed to build, validate, and operate a custom system safely. The trade-off is control and differentiation: bought solutions are faster to deploy and lower-risk operationally, but they rarely provide a defensible competitive advantage and can create vendor lock-in that unlimited budget can't fully offset.
How do you measure the ROI of an AI project that saves time but doesn't directly generate revenue?
Convert the time savings into a cost-avoidance figure — hours saved × fully-loaded labor cost — and treat that as the return, the same way you'd value any efficiency project. The subtlety is being honest about whether the saved time actually gets redeployed to other value-generating work or just becomes slack; ROI claims for efficiency-only AI projects should specify which of those two outcomes is actually expected, since only the former is real business value.
When defining scope, how do you decide what goes into the MVP vs. future phases — especially if stakeholders want it all?
Anchor the conversation in the feasibility assessment and the SMART goal's timeline, not in stakeholder preference: anything that can't be delivered with acceptable risk inside the agreed timeline and budget goes into a future phase, full stop, and that list gets written down and signed off (as in the self-driving car example). The self-driving car MVP — highway-only, daylight, good weather — shows the pattern: pick the slice that's both technically achievable and still delivers real business value on its own.
In SAFe, 50-125 people work in an Agile Release Train. How do you ensure alignment across all teams?
Through a shared ART backlog and synchronized planning cadence rather than through top-down control — every team pulls from the same prioritized feature list and delivers on the same iteration cadence, so their outputs stay compatible even though each team works semi-independently. The core values (transparency, alignment, relentless improvement, respect for people) are the cultural glue that keeps that structural mechanism from becoming just bureaucracy.
OpenAI spends roughly $700,000/day on inference. How would you optimize this cost while maintaining user experience?
The standard levers are model-side (distillation or smaller models for simpler queries, quantization, caching common responses) and routing-side (a cheap model handles easy requests, an expensive model only handles genuinely hard ones — a "model cascade"). The trade-off to watch is that aggressive cost optimization can silently degrade quality on the queries that matter most, so any optimization needs the same system + model quality metrics from Topic 3 tracked alongside the cost savings, not cost tracked in isolation.
