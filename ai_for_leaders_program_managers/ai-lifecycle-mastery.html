AI, Gen AI & AI Agents — the three layers

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. What's the actual difference between "AI" and "Generative AI" — is Gen AI a separate technology or a subset?
  2. What three things does an LLM need bolted on before it can be called an "agent" rather than just a chatbot?
  3. Can you name one everyday product for each of the three layers without looking ahead?
Hold your answers loosely — you'll check them against the recap at the end of this section.

AI is the broad umbrella: "the science that empowers machines to learn from experience, make decisions, and perform human-like tasks." Nearly everything below is a narrower slice of that umbrella.

FIG 1.1 — AI IS EVERYWHEREthree familiar surfaces

Generative AI — creates, doesn't just classify

FIG 1.2 — GENERATIVE AI: INPUT → MODEL → NEW OUTPUTnever existed before

Key insight: traditional AI recognizes patterns and classifies; Gen AI generates entirely new content that didn't exist before the prompt.

AI agents — the next frontier

FIG 1.3 — LLM + TOOLS + MEMORY + ACTIONS = AGENTautonomous over extended periods
What we just learned

AI is the umbrella science of machines that learn and decide. Generative AI is a subset focused specifically on producing new content rather than just classifying existing content. AI Agents go a layer further — an LLM becomes an agent only once it's wired up with tools (to act on the world), memory (to persist context), and the ability to take actions, letting it operate autonomously over extended periods instead of one-shot replying.

🎯 CTO view

Agents change the risk profile, not just the tech stack — an LLM that can only talk is a content risk; one that can pay, email, or write to a database is an operational risk. Budget for guardrails and audit trails before the first agent touches a production credential.

🏗️ Architect view

Design the tool boundary deliberately: which tools an agent can call, with what scopes, and what's logged on every call. Treat "tools" as a permission surface, the same way you'd treat a microservice's API contract.

📋 PM view

"We're adding an AI agent" is not one line item — it's Gen AI capability plus tool integrations plus a memory/session design. Scope and estimate each piece separately, they don't ship together.

AI vs Gen AI vs AI Agents — the hierarchy

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Are Gen AI and AI Agents siblings under the AI umbrella, or is one built on top of the other?
  2. Give one example task each system could NOT do that the next one over could.
FIG 1B.1 — THE AI HIERARCHYone umbrella, three capability tiers
TierExample task
Traditional AIIdentify whether an image is a cat or a dog
Generative AIGenerate a brand-new image of a cat that never existed before
AI AgentResearch, plan, and book a vacation based on your preferences, budget, and schedule
What we just learned

All three sit under one umbrella, but each tier adds a capability the one before it didn't have: Traditional AI recognizes and classifies; Generative AI creates net-new content; AI Agents take that generative capability and wrap it in autonomy — tools, memory, and the ability to act — so it can complete a multi-step task on its own rather than produce a single output.

🏗️ Architect view

These aren't three separate systems to choose between — they're often layered in one product. A recommendation engine (traditional AI) might feed context to a Gen AI summarizer, which an agent then acts on. Map which tier each component of your system actually belongs to before you design its interfaces.

The 7-Step AI Project Lifecycle

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Before you look at the 7 steps — how many stages would you guess a real AI project needs, start to finish?
  2. Which step do you think fails most often in practice: data, modeling, or deployment?
  3. Is this lifecycle a straight line or a loop? Why would it need to be one or the other?
FIG 2.1 — SEVEN-STEP LIFECYCLEiterative, not linear

Detailed breakdown — healthcare example

StepActionKey questionDiabetes-risk example
1Problem ScopingWhat / why important?Predict diabetes risk from patient health data
2Data AcquisitionWhat data, from where?Records, lab results, lifestyle data
3Data ExplorationClean? Missing values?Handle missing values, normalize measurements
4ModelingWhich model/architecture?Neural network, decision tree, random forest
5EvaluationAccuracy? Precision? Recall?Test on unseen patients, measure F1
6DeploymentWhere / how / API / edge?Hospital EMR system integration
7MaintenanceDrift? Retrain?Regular performance checks, retraining
What we just learned

An AI project runs through seven stages from problem scoping to maintenance — but the arrow at the end loops back to the start. It's not a waterfall you complete once; it's a cycle you keep re-entering as data, models, and business needs drift over time.

🎯 CTO view

Steps 6 and 7 are where most AI initiatives quietly die — teams get a working notebook (steps 1–5) and stop funding before deployment and monitoring. Budget deployment and maintenance as first-class line items, not an afterthought.

🏗️ Architect view

Design the retraining loop before you ship v1 — where does new labeled data land, what triggers a retrain, how does a new model get promoted. If that pipeline doesn't exist on day one, step 7 becomes manual firefighting.

📋 PM view

Because it's iterative, "done" needs a definition per cycle, not per project. Scope each loop (v1, v2…) with its own success criteria rather than treating the whole lifecycle as one deliverable with one deadline.

03Building BlocksC1V7B92

Three Ingredients: Data + Model + Compute

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If you had to drop one of Data, Model, or Compute to cut cost, which would hurt least — and why might that instinct be wrong?
  2. Can a brilliant model with poor data still ship a usable product?
FIG 3.1 — THREE EQUAL INGREDIENTSnone substitutes for another
What we just learned

Data, Model, and Compute are equally load-bearing — a great model on bad data underperforms a simple model on great data, and neither ships without enough compute to train and serve it. Treat all three as one budget line, not three separate ones you can trade off freely.

🎯 CTO view

Compute cost is the one that surprises finance teams — a training run and its ongoing inference cost should be modeled before the project is greenlit, not discovered after the first cloud bill.

🏗️ Architect view

Data quality debt compounds like technical debt — bad labels or missing governance early on shows up as unexplainable model behavior two lifecycle loops later. Invest in data pipeline design as seriously as model architecture.

04TrainingM5X3Q46

Training Process & Hyperparameters

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. What do you think happens if the learning rate is set way too high — does training just get slower, or something worse?
  2. Is more epochs always better? What's the failure mode of training for too long?
  3. Batch size 16 vs batch size 128 — which trains faster per epoch, and which uses more memory?
FIG 4.1 — TRAINING FLOWupload → train → evaluate
Epochs

Complete passes through the dataset

50 is a common Teachable Machine default. More epochs generally means better learning — up to the point of overfitting.

Batch Size

Samples processed per update

Common values: 16, 32, 64, 128. Small batches → more frequent updates, slower training. Large batches → faster per epoch, more memory.

Learning Rate

How aggressively the model adjusts

Like a gas pedal: too little and training crawls, too much and it never converges. Just right lands on stable, efficient learning.

What we just learned

Training loops the dataset through the model epoch by epoch, batch by batch, and every pass adjusts the model according to the learning rate. Get the learning rate wrong and the model either learns too slowly or never stabilizes; run too many epochs on the same data and the model starts memorizing instead of generalizing — which is exactly what the held-out test set at the end is there to catch.

🎯 CTO view

Hyperparameter search is a compute-cost multiplier — a naive grid search can burn 10-100x the training budget of a single run. Approve a search budget explicitly, don't let it happen by default.

🏗️ Architect view

Log every hyperparameter with every experiment run. Without that, "why did v3 outperform v2" becomes archaeology instead of a lookup.

05AgentsY8Z2W15

Deep Research Agents in Action

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. A human researcher takes 1-2 days on a competitor analysis. What would an agent need to do differently to get it done in minutes, not just faster typing?
  2. What's the biggest risk in trusting a 10-minute agent-generated report over a 2-day human one?
FIG 5.1 — DEEP RESEARCH AGENT WORKFLOW"research Tesla competitors"
What we just learned

A deep research agent doesn't just answer faster — it parallelizes the research itself: fanning out across dozens of sources, reading each one, then converging on a single compiled report. That's what turns a 1-2 day human task into a 5-10 minute agent task.

🎯 CTO view

Speed doesn't imply accuracy. Before this replaces a human research function, you need a citation/grounding check built into the pipeline — otherwise you've made bad information faster, not better.

📋 PM view

Treat the 5-10 minute report as a first draft that still needs a human review pass for anything customer-facing or decision-critical — budget that review time into the workflow, don't assume it's zero.

06RiskJ3H6F80

Key Challenges in AI Development

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. A model shows 99% accuracy in testing but fails in production. Name two completely different root causes for that gap.
  2. Is "model drift" a data problem, a model problem, or a world problem?
01

Data quality issues

Missing values, biased data (garbage in → garbage out), insufficient quantity, poor labeling.

02

Model bias & fairness

Models inherit biases from training data and can discriminate against groups — needs careful auditing and mitigation.

03

Real-time deployment issues

Latency requirements, scaling challenges, integration with existing systems.

04

Monitoring & maintenance

Model drift as data distributions change, performance degradation, need for regular retraining.

What we just learned

Most AI failures aren't algorithm failures — they're data, bias, latency, or drift failures. A model that tests well can still fail in production because the production data distribution, latency budget, or user population differs from what it was validated against.

🎯 CTO view

Bias audits and drift monitoring are compliance and reputational risk, not just engineering nice-to-haves — in regulated domains (finance, healthcare) they're often a hard requirement, not optional hardening.

🏗️ Architect view

Design for the 99%-in-test-fails-in-production gap explicitly: shadow deployments, canary rollout, and production-vs-training distribution monitoring should all be part of the architecture, not bolted on after the first incident.

07HorizonU7G1N54

Narrow AI → AGI → ASI

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. What's the actual test for "general" intelligence — is it about task breadth, or something deeper?
  2. If AGI can do any intellectual task a human can, what's left that's uniquely human?
FIG 7.1 — THE INTELLIGENCE ESCALATOReach level strictly broader than the last
What we just learned

Narrow AI, AGI, and ASI aren't different technologies — they're points on a widening scale of generality. Narrow AI is bounded to one task domain; AGI matches human breadth; ASI would exceed the best humans across every domain and, per the theory, begin improving itself.

🎯 CTO view

None of today's roadmap decisions should be blocked on AGI arriving — plan around narrow-AI capability curves you can actually forecast, and treat AGI/ASI timelines as a watch-list, not a dependency.

08Applied PracticeO2K9D37

Applied Practice: Diabetes Risk Prediction

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. For a diabetes-risk model, would you optimize harder for precision (avoid false alarms) or recall (avoid missed cases) — and why?
  2. What compliance constraint is unique to healthcare deployment that wouldn't apply to, say, a movie recommender?

Walking the 7-step lifecycle end-to-end against a single real scenario:

1 · Scoping
Predict patient diabetes risk. Why: early detection enables early treatment. Stakeholders: patients, doctors, insurers.
2 · Data
Demographics (age, BMI), lab results (blood sugar, insulin), lifestyle data (diet, exercise, smoking). Sourced from hospital EMR and patient questionnaires.
3 · Prep
Handle missing values, normalize/scale features, engineer BMI from height/weight, 80/20 train/test split.
4 · Modeling
Candidates: Random Forest (interpretable), Neural Network (high performance), Logistic Regression (baseline), XGBoost.
5 · Evaluation
Accuracy, Precision (false positives matter), Recall (false negatives are dangerous in disease detection), F1-score.
6 · Deployment
Integrate with hospital EMR, secure API, doctor-facing UI, HIPAA compliance required.
7 · Maintenance
Monitor drift as population health changes, retrain regularly, audit for demographic bias, keep documentation current.
What we just learned

Applied to a real scenario, the 7 abstract steps become concrete decisions — which features to engineer, which metric to optimize (recall over raw accuracy, given the cost of a missed diagnosis), and which regulatory constraint (HIPAA) shapes the deployment step specifically.

🎯 CTO view

In healthcare, recall usually outweighs precision — a missed diabetes case is more costly than a false alarm that leads to a confirmatory test. Make that trade-off an explicit, documented decision, not an accident of the default threshold.

🏗️ Architect view

HIPAA-driven deployment means the API, logging, and data-at-rest design all need compliance review before step 6 starts — not a retrofit after the model works.

📋 PM view

Bias auditing across demographics (step 7) is its own workstream with its own timeline — don't fold it silently into "maintenance," it needs a named owner and a recurring cadence.

ReferenceL4B8M61

Final architect's perspective

As an AI architect, I always remind my teams

"The model is not the product — the solution is."

Architecture is about:

  • End-to-end thinking — not just model accuracy
  • System reliability — not just research results
  • Business value — not just technical novelty
FIG 9.1 — THE ITERATIVE ARCHITECT'S LOOPresearch to learning, and back again

Always ask:

  • "What could go wrong?"
  • "How does this serve the user?"
  • "Can we measure success?"
  • "How do we handle edge cases?"
🏗️ The one line to remember

Building AI systems is roughly 80% data engineering, 20% modeling — and architecture is the discipline of never letting the 20% distract from the 80%.

Active RecallX6P3C95

Test yourself — deep thought questions

If AI models learn from historical data, are they inherently conservative — reinforcing the past rather than being genuinely innovative?
Largely yes, by construction: a model's outputs are a function of the patterns present in its training data, so it tends to reproduce and interpolate within that distribution rather than originate something outside it. "Novelty" in generated output is usually recombination of existing patterns in a new configuration — impressive, but not the same as a human insight that breaks from precedent.
In the 7-step lifecycle, is data acquisition always the step with the most downstream impact if done poorly?
It's the most common failure point, but not universally the most impactful one — a scoping failure (solving the wrong problem) can waste the entire cycle regardless of data quality, and a deployment failure can sink a technically sound model just as completely. Data quality dominates in practice because it's the easiest step to underinvest in, not because it's structurally guaranteed to matter most.
Can we truly build unbiased AI systems, or are we just shifting human bias into the machine?
Mostly the latter — a model trained on human-generated or human-labeled data inherits the biases embedded in that data and in the labeling process. "Debiasing" techniques can reduce specific, measured disparities, but they don't remove bias as a category; they trade one set of trade-offs for another, which is why bias auditing has to be continuous rather than a one-time fix.
If AI agents can act autonomously — making payments, sending emails — who is legally liable when something goes wrong?
This is genuinely unsettled and varies by jurisdiction, but current practice generally places liability on the deploying organization and/or developer rather than the agent itself, since the agent has no legal personhood. That's precisely why the tool-permission boundary (what an agent is allowed to touch, and what's logged) matters operationally — it's the evidence trail that liability arguments will hinge on.
A model shows 99% accuracy on test data but fails in production — what's actually going on?
Usually one of: the production data distribution differs from the test set (distribution shift), the test set leaked information from training (data leakage inflating the reported number), the model overfit to quirks of the test set through repeated tuning, or production introduces edge cases the test set never covered. Accuracy on a static test set is a necessary check, not a guarantee of real-world generalization.
How should we measure "success" for an AI system making high-stakes decisions differently than we'd measure a human decision-maker?
A human decision is usually judged case-by-case with room for context and appeal; an AI system is judged in aggregate — across thousands of decisions, for consistency, calibration, and disparate impact across groups, not just whether any single decision felt reasonable. That means success metrics need a fairness/consistency axis (does it treat similar cases similarly, across demographics) alongside raw accuracy, which isn't typically how we'd score an individual human's judgment.
If we achieve AGI, should it have rights or moral consideration?
There's no consensus — the honest answer is that this depends entirely on unresolved questions about whether the system has anything like subjective experience, which we currently have no reliable way to test for. The more actionable near-term framing many ethicists use is precautionary: as systems become more capable and more autonomous, the cost of being wrong in either direction (over-attributing or under-attributing moral status) grows, which argues for taking the question seriously well before the technology forces the issue.
The AI lifecycle is iterative — so how do you know when to stop iterating and ship?
In practice, you stop when the model clears a pre-agreed, business-defined threshold (not an ever-improving research metric) and the cost of another iteration exceeds its expected value — diminishing returns on accuracy, or a ticking business deadline, outweigh further gains. The mistake is letting "we could always keep improving it" delay shipping indefinitely; that's why step 1 (problem scoping) should define the success bar up front, so step 5 (evaluation) has a stopping condition instead of an open-ended target.
Should models be optimized for accuracy or interpretability in life-critical applications?
It's rarely a strict either/or — the real question is what interpretability buys you: in healthcare or lending, an explainable model lets a human reviewer catch a wrong-for-the-right-reasons or right-for-the-wrong-reasons prediction before it causes harm, which raw accuracy alone can't guarantee. Many teams resolve this by using a highly accurate model but pairing it with a separate interpretability layer (e.g. SHAP/LIME explanations) rather than sacrificing accuracy for a simpler but weaker model outright.
