01FoundationsP2K8W35

Introduction to AI Models & Algorithms

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If a model is "just a mathematical equation," what do you think actually makes it feel intelligent?
  2. Guess before reading: how many times has training compute grown since 2010 — 100x? 10,000x? A billion times?

A model is a mathematical equation — a bunch of math trained to recognize patterns. The human brain has 86 billion neurons firing electrical and chemical signals; an AI model is an artificial neural network doing the mathematical equivalent. We mimic the brain mathematically, not biologically.

FIG 1.1 — COMPUTE GROWTH SINCE 2010doubling every 5–6 months
What we just learned

A model's apparent "intelligence" is complex pattern matching expressed as math — millions or trillions of weighted connections mimicking, at a mathematical level, what neurons do biologically. That math has scaled almost unimaginably fast: training compute has grown roughly 10 billion-fold since 2010, doubling every 5-6 months, faster than Moore's Law ever moved for hardware.

🎯 CTO view

A growth curve doubling every 5-6 months will outrun almost any fixed infrastructure plan — build compute strategy around elastic cloud capacity, not a static hardware purchase sized for today's model.

🏗️ Architect view

The most likely limiting factor isn't hardware — it's energy and data availability. High-quality training data is finite and expensive to generate faster than compute is scaling; plan data strategy (see Study Note 024) as the actual bottleneck, not compute.

Prime yourself — answer before reading on

  2. Why does training need a "backward" pass at all — why can't the network just get it right on the forward pass?

One full pass through the entire dataset.

06

Number of samples processed at once.

07

Speed of weight updates.

08

How far predictions are from ground truth.

FIG 2.2 — THE TRAINING LOOPrepeats every epoch
What we just learned

A neural network is layers of neurons connected by learned weights: an epoch is one full pass through the whole dataset, a batch is the chunk of samples processed before each weight update. Training needs a backward pass because the forward pass alone only produces a guess and a measurable error (loss) — backpropagation is the mechanism that computes how much each individual weight contributed to that error, so the optimizer knows which direction to nudge each one.

🏗️ Architect view

Batch size isn't just a training-speed knob — it directly affects memory footprint on the GPU. Architecture decisions about hardware (Topic 8) and batch size are coupled, not independent choices.

03Hands-OnJ9D4X61

Building a Simple NN — TensorFlow Playground

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If a network with 100 hidden neurons performs well, does that automatically mean 100 is the "right" number? What would make you suspect otherwise?

TensorFlow Playground is a no-code, browser-based way to build and train a neural network visually — drag layers, adjust hyperparameters, watch weights update live, and see the decision boundary form between two classes in real time.

Learning rate

Too high → unstable training. Too low → slow learning.

Batch size

Number of samples per weight update.

Noise

Amount of randomness injected into the data.

Train/test split

50/50, 80/20, etc.

Architecture

Number of hidden layers and neurons per layer.

What we just learned

Playground makes hyperparameter tuning tangible — you can watch a decision boundary form and immediately see the effect of learning rate, noise, or architecture size. A model performing well at 100 neurons doesn't prove 100 is optimal: the right test is whether a smaller network (fewer neurons/layers) achieves comparably good performance — if it does, the larger one was likely overparameterized for the task, wasting compute and risking overfitting.

🎯 CTO view

"It works" is not the same question as "it's efficiently sized" — unnecessarily large architectures cost more to train and serve indefinitely. Push teams to demonstrate the smallest architecture that hits the target metric, not just any architecture that does.

04HierarchyN5T7B28

Model Architecture Hierarchy

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Where do CNNs, Transformers, and GANs sit in the AI → ML → learning-type hierarchy — are they a fourth branch, or do they sit inside the existing three?
FIG 4.1 — AI → ML → LEARNING TYPES → ALGORITHMSwhere deep architectures fit
What we just learned

CNNs, RNNs, Transformers, and GANs aren't a separate fourth category — they're specific architectures of deep neural networks, which itself is one implementation approach that can be applied within supervised, unsupervised, or even reinforcement learning depending on how they're trained. A Transformer trained on labeled translation pairs is supervised; a GAN's generator/discriminator setup is its own distinct training dynamic layered on top of that same hierarchy.

🏗️ Architect view

When scoping a project, separate "which learning paradigm" (supervised/unsupervised/RL) from "which architecture" (CNN/Transformer/etc.) — they're independent decisions, and conflating them narrows your options before you've actually reasoned about the problem.

05ArchitecturesG3H6Y94

Neural Network Types in Detail

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. RNNs process sequentially and "forget" long-term context (vanishing gradient). What problem was LSTM specifically built to fix?
  2. Transformers process all tokens in parallel instead of sequentially. Why is that a genuine breakthrough rather than just a speed trick?
FIG 5.1 — RNN: FEEDBACK LOOPoutput depends on input AND previous state
FNN / MLP

Feed-forward

One-direction flow, simple 1:1 mapping. Use: tabular classification/regression.

CNN

Convolutional

Convolution layers extract features, pooling reduces dimensions. Use: image classification, object detection.

RNN

Recurrent

Feedback loops — output depends on input and prior state. Use: time series, speech. Problem: vanishing gradient (forgets long-term).

LSTM

Long Short-Term Memory

Enhanced RNN with "memory cells" that solve the vanishing gradient problem. Use: stock prediction, translation, text generation.

Transformers

Self-attention

Processes all tokens simultaneously, in parallel. Powers ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama. From "Attention Is All You Need" (Google, 2017).

GANs

Generative Adversarial

Two networks compete — Generator creates fakes, Discriminator detects them. Use: image generation, deepfakes, data augmentation.

Autoencoders

Compress & reconstruct

Encoder compresses, decoder reconstructs — learns efficient representations. Use: anomaly detection, denoising, dimensionality reduction.

ArchitectureKey featureBest use cases
Feed-Forward (MLP)One-way flow, simpleTabular data, basic classification/regression
CNNFeature extraction via convolutionImage recognition, object detection
RNNFeedback loops (memory)Time series, speech — but forgets long-term
LSTMLong-term memory cellsStock prediction, translation, text generation
TransformersSelf-attention, parallelLLMs, chatbots, translation, NLP
GANsTwo networks competingImage generation, deepfakes, augmentation
AutoencodersCompression & reconstructionAnomaly detection, denoising, compression
What we just learned

LSTM was purpose-built to fix RNN's vanishing gradient problem — its memory cells preserve long-term dependencies that a plain RNN loses over long sequences. Transformers' parallelism is a genuine breakthrough, not just speed: processing sequentially forces RNN-style models to compress everything through a bottleneck one token at a time, while self-attention lets every token directly reference every other token at once, which is both faster to train and better at capturing long-range relationships.

🏗️ Architect view

Match the architecture to the data's actual structure, not the current trend — Transformers dominate NLP headlines, but a CNN is still usually the right, cheaper choice for pure image classification, and a plain FNN is fine for a tabular regression problem.

06EvaluationV8L1Q57

Model Evaluation: Classification Metrics

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. A fraud model hits 99% accuracy but the fraud rate is only 1%. Should you be impressed? Why or why not?
  2. For a self-driving car's pedestrian detector, would you rather minimize false positives or false negatives?
FIG 6.1 — CONFUSION MATRIXthe foundation of every classification metric
Accuracy

(TP+TN) / Total

Overall correctness. Good for balanced data, misleading for imbalanced data.

Precision

TP / (TP+FP)

"Of all positive predictions, how many were correct?" Minimizes false positives. E.g. spam detection.

Recall

TP / (TP+FN)

"Of all actual positives, how many did we catch?" Minimizes false negatives. E.g. disease detection.

F1-Score

Harmonic mean

2·(P·R)/(P+R). Balances precision and recall — best for imbalanced datasets.

Practical example: fraud detection (bank, 1,000 transactions)

Predicted fraudPredicted not fraud
Actual fraud70 (TP)30 (FN)
Actual not fraud50 (FP)850 (TN)
MetricCalculationValue
Accuracy(70+850)/100092%
Precision70/(70+50)58.3%
Recall70/(70+30)70%
💡 Which metric matters most?
The bank prioritizes precision — false positives block legitimate transactions and frustrate customers (e.g. a card declined abroad), which is a direct customer-experience cost even though it looks like "only" a 92% accuracy story.
What we just learned

Every classification metric is built from the same four confusion-matrix cells. Accuracy alone is dangerous on imbalanced data — a 99%-accurate fraud model could just be predicting "not fraud" every time if fraud is rare, catching zero real cases while still scoring well. Precision and recall trade off against each other, and which one to prioritize is a business decision, not a technical one: a spam filter favors precision (don't block real email), a disease or pedestrian detector favors recall (don't miss a real positive).

🎯 CTO view

Never let a team report "99% accuracy" as the headline metric without also stating the class balance — on a 1%-positive-rate problem, that number alone tells you almost nothing about whether the model works.

📋 PM view

Get the precision/recall trade-off written into the success metrics before modeling starts (see the SMART goals topic in the PM notes) — deciding it after seeing the results invites the team to rationalize whichever number came out better.

07EvaluationD4W9F26

Model Evaluation: Regression Metrics

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If RMSE is $27,000 for house price predictions, does that mean every single prediction is off by exactly $27K?
  2. What's the actual difference between R-squared and accuracy — are they measuring the same thing in different units?
FIG 7.1 — LINEAR REGRESSIONy = mx + b, residuals are the errors
MAE

Mean Absolute Error

Average magnitude of errors. Easy to interpret, robust to outliers.

MSE

Mean Squared Error

Penalizes large errors heavily. Not in original units, sensitive to outliers.

RMSE

√MSE

Interpretable in the same units as output — "on average, off by $X." Best for business communication.

R²

Variance explained

0 to 1, higher is better. 80% = 80% of output variation explained by the input(s).

Practical example: house price prediction

💡 Best metric for stakeholders
RMSE — it's the one that translates directly into a sentence a VP can repeat: "the model forecasts within about $27K on average."
What we just learned

RMSE of $27,000 is an average, not a per-prediction guarantee — individual errors range from $10K to $50K in the example, and RMSE (because it squares errors before averaging) is pulled upward by the larger ones more than MAE would be. R-squared is not the same thing as accuracy: accuracy counts correct classifications, while R² measures how much of the output's variance the model explains — an 80% R² means the model accounts for 80% of the pattern in price variation, not that 80% of predictions were "correct" in any classification sense.

📋 PM view

When reporting model performance upward, lead with RMSE in the metric's real units, not R² or MSE — a VP can act on "off by $27K" immediately; "R² of 0.8" requires a follow-up explanation every time.

What we just learned

TPUs push that specialization even further for TensorFlow workloads specifically, at the cost of vendor lock-in to Google Cloud — a real trade-off between performance and platform flexibility, not a free upgrade.

🎯 CTO view

Cloud's pay-as-you-go model is usually the right default for training (bursty, high peak demand); local/on-prem can make sense for inference at steady, predictable volume where the capex pays back. Don't apply one model to both phases by default.

10EfficiencyB5N2Y86

Transfer Learning

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If you're detecting tumors in X-rays and the pre-trained model only ever saw everyday photos, would transfer learning still help — or is the domain gap too big?

Definition: reusing a model trained on one task as a starting point for another, similar task.

Analogy: if you can skate, you can learn skiing faster — you already have balance, comfort on slippery surfaces, and weight distribution instincts. That knowledge transfers to a similar-but-different task.

FIG 10.1 — TRANSFER LEARNING WITH A CNNfreeze the feature extractor, train only the classifier
Speed

Training time drops from hours to minutes.

Data

Requires far less data — the feature extractor already learned good general features.

Compute

Less compute needed — no need to retrain everything from scratch.

Performance

Often better, because the base model was pre-trained on a massive dataset.

What we just learned

Transfer learning works by freezing the general-purpose feature extractor from a pre-trained model and training only new final layers on your specific data — dramatically cutting time, data, and compute needs. The tumor/X-ray case is exactly the edge case worth flagging: when the target domain is very different from the source (medical imaging vs. everyday photos), the low-level features (edges, textures) still often transfer usefully, but you may need to unfreeze and fine-tune more layers than usual, or accept a smaller benefit than a same-domain transfer would give.

🏗️ Architect view

How many layers to unfreeze is a spectrum, not binary — start with only the final layer trainable; if performance is insufficient, progressively unfreeze earlier layers with a lower learning rate, watching for overfitting as you go, rather than defaulting to fine-tuning the whole network.

Active RecallX7F5N61

Test yourself — deep thought questions

If a neural network with 100 neurons performs as well as one with 1000, which should you choose? What factors matter besides accuracy?
Choose the smaller one, all else equal — it trains faster, costs less to serve at inference time, and is less prone to overfitting on future data. Beyond accuracy, weigh training/inference cost, latency requirements, interpretability (smaller models are often easier to inspect), and how much headroom you need for the model to keep performing as real-world data drifts from the training distribution.
Transformers process all tokens in parallel with no recurrence. Does that mean they're "less intelligent" than RNNs, which process sequentially?
No — sequential processing isn't a proxy for intelligence, it's just one architectural strategy for handling order. Transformers actually capture long-range dependencies better than RNNs precisely because self-attention lets every token reference every other token directly, rather than passing information through a compressed hidden state one step at a time. Parallelism is a training/inference efficiency and modeling-power advantage, not a step down in capability.
In fraud detection, the bank prioritizes precision over recall. But what if the bank is losing millions to undetected fraud? Should they shift to recall?
The right answer depends on quantifying both costs directly — the dollar cost of missed fraud (false negatives) versus the cost of blocked legitimate transactions and customer churn (false positives) — and choosing the threshold that minimizes total expected cost, not defaulting to either metric by habit. If undetected fraud losses genuinely exceed the customer-experience cost of more false positives, that's a signal to shift the decision threshold toward recall, ideally validated with real financial data rather than intuition.
Transfer learning works well when domains are similar. What if you're detecting tumors in X-rays and the pre-trained model was trained on natural images? Would transfer learning still help?
Usually yes, but with a caveat — the earliest CNN layers learn very general features (edges, textures, gradients) that transfer reasonably well even across very different domains like natural photos and X-rays. The benefit shrinks the deeper you go, since later layers encode increasingly domain-specific patterns (like "dog ear" shapes) that don't apply to medical imaging — so the practical approach is to keep early layers frozen, unfreeze and fine-tune more of the later layers, and expect a smaller boost than a same-domain transfer would give.
Open-source LLMs are free but require expertise. Proprietary LLMs are paid but easier. For a non-tech company, which is more cost-effective long-term?
For most non-tech companies, proprietary APIs are more cost-effective — the "free" cost of open source is misleading once you account for the ML engineering and MLOps expertise needed to deploy, secure, and maintain it reliably, expertise a non-tech company typically doesn't have in-house and would need to hire or consult for. The calculus flips only at genuinely large, predictable usage volumes where the API's per-token cost eventually exceeds the cost of an internal team.
LLM benchmarks show models scoring >90% on medical exams. But can they replace doctors? What's missing from benchmark evaluations?
Benchmarks measure recall of medical knowledge under exam conditions — they don't measure clinical judgment under uncertainty, physical examination, reading a patient's non-verbal cues, taking legal and ethical accountability for a decision, or handling the countless edge cases and comorbidities that don't fit neatly into an exam question. A high exam score is a necessary but nowhere near sufficient signal for replacing the actual practice of medicine.
Compute doubles every 5-6 months. What happens when we have effectively infinite compute? Will we just build larger models forever?
Almost certainly not indefinitely — data availability, energy costs, and diminishing returns on scale (each further doubling has historically bought smaller capability gains) are more likely to bind before compute itself runs out. The field has already started shifting some effort toward efficiency (smaller models matching larger ones, per Topic 3's "quality over size" theme) rather than purely chasing scale, suggesting compute won't be the only lever pulled going forward.
GANs can generate realistic fake images. How can we distinguish real from fake? Should we develop detection systems or focus on regulation?
Both, and neither alone is sufficient — detection systems (often themselves ML models trained to spot generation artifacts) are in a constant arms race with generators that improve specifically to evade them, so detection alone degrades over time. Regulation (mandatory content provenance/watermarking, disclosure requirements) addresses the incentive structure rather than trying to win a technical arms race, which is why most serious proposals combine both: technical detection as a first line of defense, and policy/provenance standards as the more durable backstop.
