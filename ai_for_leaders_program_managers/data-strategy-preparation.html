What we just learned

Data, Model, and Compute are equally necessary, but data alone is called "the fuel" because a great algorithm and unlimited GPUs produce nothing without it — and that dependency shows up directly in how time gets spent: roughly 80% of a real AI project goes into preparing data, only 20% into training and tuning the model itself.

🎯 CTO view

If your team's time allocation doesn't roughly match 80/20, that's a signal worth investigating — either the data pipeline is unusually mature, or (far more likely) data quality problems are about to surface downstream in evaluation or production.

📋 PM view

Timeline estimates that don't explicitly budget 80% of the schedule for data work are the single most common reason AI project timelines slip — pad the data phase, not the modeling phase.

What Is a Data Strategy?

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. What's the difference between "having data" and "having a data strategy"?
  2. Which regulation carries a fine of up to 4% of global revenue — and why would that make data strategy a board-level topic?

Definition: a comprehensive plan that defines how an organization will collect, manage, govern, share, and use data to support AI projects.

01

Enables reliable model training

Without a strategy, models are built on quicksand.

02

Reduces bias, privacy & governance risk

Prevents reputational damage and legal exposure.

03

Ensures reusability across teams

Data becomes an asset, not a one-time cost.

04

Supports regulatory compliance

GDPR fines: up to €20M or 4% of global revenue. HIPAA: health data protection requirements.

05

Aligns AI with business goals

Translates data capabilities into business value and KPIs.

What we just learned

Having data isn't the same as having a data strategy — a strategy is the deliberate plan for how data gets collected, governed, shared, and reused across the organization. It matters for five concrete reasons, and one of them (GDPR's 4%-of-global-revenue fine) turns data strategy from an engineering concern into a board-level risk conversation.

🎯 CTO view

Treat data governance failures the same way you'd treat a security breach in your risk register — a GDPR fine at 4% of global revenue can dwarf the cost of the AI project it was supporting.

9 Key Components of a Data Strategy

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. Before reading — list 3 things you think a complete data strategy has to cover beyond "collect data."
1

Data discovery & audit

Identify existing sources, evaluate availability/quality/gaps — what do we already have?

2

Data collection plan

How will we collect it — sensors, APIs, manual, logs? What frequency — real-time, daily, batch?

3

Data quality management

Define quality metrics: accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity.

4

Data governance

Define roles and responsibilities, implement versioning — who owns and manages the data?

5

Data privacy & compliance

Adhere to GDPR/HIPAA, anonymize PII, implement consent management.

6

Data storage & access

Data lake vs warehouse, access levels via principle of least privilege.

7

Data labeling

For supervised learning — labeling guidelines, cost ($0.08–$3.00/object), quality control.

8

Data integration

Merge datasets from multiple sources, build ETL pipelines, ensure consistent formatting.

9

Data strategy KPIs

Model accuracy improvements, time-to-insight reduction, data accessibility scores.

What we just learned

A complete data strategy spans nine components, from discovery all the way to KPIs that prove it's working — and notably, "collect the data" is only one of nine, with governance, privacy, storage, and labeling each demanding just as much deliberate planning.

🏗️ Architect view

Components 4-6 (governance, privacy, storage) are the ones that need to be architected before data starts flowing — retrofitting access control and PII anonymization onto a live data lake is far more disruptive than designing them in from component 1.

  1. Most of the world's data is unstructured, yet enterprise data strategy is still dominated by structured databases. Why the gap?
Structured dataUnstructured data
Organized in a predefined schema — rows/columns. Easy to store, access, analyze. Examples: Excel, CSV, SQL tables, Pandas DataFrames.No predefined data model. Requires pre-processing to extract features. Examples: text, images, audio, video. Formats: JSON, MP4, MP3, PDF.

Unstructured examples: free text ("The patient reported chest pain and shortness of breath"), raw image pixel data with no schema, or semi-structured JSON like {"patient_id": 123, "symptoms": ["chest_pain", "fatigue"]}.

What we just learned

Structured data fits neatly into rows and columns and is cheap to query; unstructured data — text, images, audio, video — carries no schema and needs pre-processing before a model can use it. Traditional databases still dominate enterprise strategy mostly because structured data is cheaper and faster to govern and query, even though unstructured data is the larger and faster-growing share of what organizations actually generate.

🏗️ Architect view

Design storage by data type, not by department — structured data belongs in a warehouse for fast queries, unstructured belongs in a lake, and a system that forces both through the same schema-on-write store will bottleneck on the unstructured side.

  1. Why is labeled data described as "higher quality but limited scale" while unlabeled is "abundant but less structured"?

Data WITH associated tags/labels. Required for supervised learning. Expensive to create (human labeling). Higher quality, limited scale.Data WITHOUT tags/labels. Used for unsupervised learning. Easy to access, no manual labeling. Abundant but less structured.
Labeled examples
  • 🖼️ Image of a dog → label: "Dog"
  • 📧 "Win free iPhone" → label: "Spam"
  • 🏦 $500 transaction → label: "Legitimate"
Unlabeled examples
  • 🖼️ Image → no label
  • 📧 "Meeting at 3pm" → no label

What we just learned

The trade-off is direct: labeling requires human effort, so labeled datasets are smaller and pricier but power supervised learning's precision; unlabeled data costs nothing extra to collect, so it's abundant and feeds unsupervised methods that find structure without being told the answer in advance.

📋 PM view

Before committing to a supervised approach, check whether the labeled data actually exists or has to be created — that decision alone can move a project's budget by 10x (see the labeling cost topic later).

06Learning TypesR9C2Y58

Supervised, Unsupervised & Reinforcement Learning

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. A baby learning not to touch fire isn't shown thousands of labeled "hot/not hot" examples. What kind of learning is that instead?
  2. What do clustering and dimensionality reduction have in common that puts them both under "unsupervised"?
FIG 6.1 — SUPERVISED LEARNINGinput → model → known-type output

Use cases: email spam detection, fraud detection, diabetes risk prediction.

FIG 6.2 — UNSUPERVISED LEARNINGfind structure with no labels given

Use cases: customer segmentation, anomaly detection, dimensionality reduction.

FIG 6.3 — REINFORCEMENT LEARNINGlearn by trial and error, maximize cumulative reward

Example: a baby learning not to touch fire — touch fire → −10 (penalty), drink milk → +10 (reward); the agent learns to maximize cumulative reward. Use cases: game playing, robotics, self-driving cars.

What we just learned

Supervised learning needs labels to map input to a known output; unsupervised learning finds structure — clusters, reduced dimensions — in data with no labels at all; reinforcement learning needs neither, learning instead from a reward signal generated by interacting with an environment. The baby-and-fire example is RL because there's no pre-labeled dataset of "hot" and "not hot" — the feedback (pain) arrives after the action, exactly like a reward signal.

🏗️ Architect view

The learning type you choose constrains your data strategy long before modeling starts — committing to supervised learning means committing to a labeling budget and pipeline; committing to RL means building a safe simulation environment before any real-world trial and error is acceptable.

Practical example: spam classification

✅ Good data
  • "Win a free iPhone now!" → spam ✅
  • "Meeting agenda attached" → not_spam ✅
  • "Get cheap meds online" → spam ✅
  • "Your Amazon order shipped" → not_spam ✅

50/50 balance, correct labels, no duplicates.

❌ Bad data
  • "Win a free iPhone now!" → not_spam ❌ (wrong)
  • "Meeting agenda attached" → spam ❌ (wrong)
  • "Your Amazon order shipped" → not_spam (duplicate row)
  • NULL → NULL ❌ (missing)

Unbalanced (2 spam / 4 not_spam), wrong labels, missing data.

What we just learned

Data quality isn't one property — it's nine dimensions (relevance, representation, accuracy, completeness, balance, consistency, timeliness, privacy, signal-to-noise), and the spam example shows how a dataset can fail on several at once: wrong labels, a duplicate row, a missing entry, and a class imbalance, all in six rows. Hidden bias is dangerous specifically because it can pass a test set that shares the same bias — which is why representation and balance need to be checked against real-world distribution, not just against the test split.

🎯 CTO view

Research increasingly shows quality beats quantity (see Topic 14) — don't default to "collect more data" as the fix for a struggling model without first auditing against these nine dimensions.

🏗️ Architect view

Bias hidden in both train and test sets is invisible to standard evaluation — the mitigation is an independent, out-of-distribution validation set or real-world A/B testing, not just a bigger held-out split from the same source.

09PreparationN2X5T94

Data Wrangling & Feature Engineering

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. What's the difference between "wrangling" and "feature engineering" — is it just two names for the same cleanup work?

Data wrangling — cleaning and organizing raw data

  1. Read raw data from multiple sources
  2. Handle missing values (drop or impute)
  3. Remove duplicates
  4. Fix inconsistent formatting
  5. Merge/join datasets
  6. Convert data types
  7. Validate data quality

Feature engineering — transforming features to improve model performance

1

Feature selection

Remove irrelevant/noisy columns; drop highly correlated features (>0.95).

2

Feature scaling

Normalize Age (20-80) to 0-1; standardize Income ($0-$1M) to mean=0, σ=1.

3

Feature creation

BMI = Weight/Height². Debt-to-Income = Loan/Income. Age Group = Young/Middle/Senior.

4

Encoding categorical data

One-hot encoding: City → [NY, CHI, SF]. Label encoding: Risk → [Low, Med, High].

Before / after: cleaning messy data

What we just learned

Wrangling and feature engineering are sequential, not interchangeable: wrangling makes raw, messy data usable at all (fix errors, remove duplicates, handle missing values), while feature engineering takes that clean data and reshapes it specifically to help a model learn better (scaling, encoding, creating derived features like BMI). You wrangle first, then engineer.

🏗️ Architect view

Correlation-based feature selection (dropping features >0.95 correlated) needs to happen after wrangling, not before — correlations computed on dirty data (with the negative-age row still in) can be misleading.

What we just learned

Labeling cost scales dramatically with task complexity — a simple classification label runs about a cent, while pixel-level semantic segmentation runs nearly a dollar and 3D point cloud data hits $3 per frame, a 250x spread. At self-driving-car scale, that spread is the difference between a manageable budget and tens of millions of dollars.

🎯 CTO view

Before committing to a labeling-heavy task type (segmentation, point cloud), price it out at your expected data volume first — the per-object cost multiplier can single-handedly make or break a project's budget.

📋 PM view

Active learning and semi-supervised approaches (label only the most informative samples, let the model bootstrap the rest) are the standard cost-reduction lever here — worth evaluating before defaulting to labeling the entire dataset.

What we just learned

The three storage layers optimize for different jobs: databases for fast transactional reads/writes, warehouses for structured analytics and BI reporting, and lakes for holding every data type — structured, text, images, audio, video — for ML and AI work. A "data swamp" happens when a lake accumulates data of every type with no governance, catalog, or ownership, so nobody can find, trust, or safely use what's in it anymore.

🏗️ Architect view

The minimum governance that keeps a lake from becoming a swamp: a data catalog (what's here, who owns it), consistent naming/partitioning conventions, and access control from day one — retrofitting a catalog onto years of ungoverned data is a multi-month project on its own.

🎯 CTO view

Choosing lake vs warehouse isn't either/or for most orgs — the common pattern is a lake for raw/all-type storage feeding a warehouse for the structured, analytics-ready subset. Budget and architect for both, not one instead of the other.

Pipelines, Security & Governance

Prime yourself — answer before reading on
  1. If a company stores PII in a data lake without encryption and a breach occurs, who do you think is actually liable — the data engineer, the architect, or the CEO?
FIG 13.1 — END-TO-END DATA PIPELINEraw data to monitored model
1

Access control

Principle of least privilege, RBAC, multi-factor authentication.

2

Data encryption

At-rest encryption, in-transit encryption (TLS/SSL).

3

Data quality monitoring

Track missing values over time, monitor drift, validate schema changes.

4

Data lineage

Track origin, track transformations applied, audit trail for compliance.

What we just learned

The ETL pipeline (Extract, Transform, Load) is the backbone that moves raw data into a trained, monitored model, and security/governance wraps around every stage of it — access control and encryption protect the data itself, while quality monitoring and lineage protect your ability to trust and audit it later. Liability for a PII breach in practice spreads across roles (the architect who designed access, the engineer who implemented it, leadership accountable for governance) rather than resting on one person — which is exactly why governance needs to be a named, owned responsibility, not an assumption.

🎯 CTO view

"Who's liable" is the wrong first question — the better one is "who owns encryption-at-rest as a checklist item before launch." Assign that ownership explicitly; don't let it default to whoever happens to touch the data lake last.

🏗️ Architect view

Data lineage is the component teams skip under time pressure and regret first during an audit or an incident — build it into the pipeline from day one, since retrofitting lineage tracking onto years of untracked transformations is close to impossible.

Active RecallX4T8D26

Test yourself — deep thought questions

If data quality is so critical, why do most companies still treat data as a "cost center" rather than a "strategic asset"? What would it take to change this?
Data work is largely invisible in a P&L — cleaning and governance don't show up as a line item the way a new product feature does, so it gets funded reactively (after a failure) rather than proactively. Changing this usually requires tying data quality metrics directly to a business KPI the leadership team already tracks (e.g. "data completeness → model accuracy → conversion rate"), so the asset's value becomes visible in the same language as the rest of the business.
In the self-driving car case, we need to label millions of images. At $0.08 per reviewed object, how can we reduce costs — active learning? semi-supervised?
Both help, and they compound: active learning has the model flag the examples it's most uncertain about, so humans only label the highest-value samples instead of everything; semi-supervised learning then lets the model bootstrap from a smaller labeled set plus a much larger unlabeled pool. In practice, teams also use pre-trained foundation models to auto-label first drafts and have humans only correct errors, which is far cheaper than labeling from scratch.
Structured data is easy to analyze but unstructured data is exploding. How should companies balance their data strategy between the two?
Rather than picking one, most mature strategies route by use case: keep structured data in a warehouse for fast, governed BI queries, and route unstructured data into a lake feeding ML pipelines that can extract structure from it (embeddings, entity extraction) on the way in. The balance point is usually "extract structure at ingestion where cheap, leave it raw where extraction cost isn't justified yet."
GDPR fines can be 4% of global revenue. If a company stores PII in a Data Lake without encryption, who's liable — the Data Engineer, the Data Architect, or the CEO?
Legally, GDPR liability generally attaches to the organization (the "data controller"), and regulators pursue the company rather than an individual employee in most cases — but internally, accountability typically traces to whoever owned the governance decision, which is usually the Data Architect's design and leadership's sign-off on risk acceptance, not the engineer who implemented what they were told to build. This is exactly why governance ownership needs to be explicit and documented, not assumed.
Research shows 30% high-quality data can match 100% noisy data. Does this mean we should stop collecting more data and focus only on quality? What's the trade-off?
Not entirely — the finding is that quality can substitute for quantity up to a point, not that quantity has zero value. More diverse, high-quality data still helps with edge cases and rare scenarios that a smaller pruned set may simply not contain. The practical trade-off is diminishing returns: past a certain quality threshold, additional volume yields less benefit than the cost of curating it better, so the right move is usually "prune aggressively first, then decide if more collection is still worth it" rather than an absolute stop.
If AI models are built on historical data, are they inherently conservative — reinforcing the past? How can we intentionally inject novelty?
Largely yes, for the same reason discussed in the AI Lifecycle notes — a model's outputs are bounded by the patterns in its training distribution. Injecting novelty deliberately usually means synthetic data generation, deliberately oversampling underrepresented or emerging patterns, or human-in-the-loop curation that intentionally includes edge cases the historical data underrepresents, rather than hoping novelty emerges on its own.
In reinforcement learning, the agent learns by trial and error. In the real world, trial and error can be dangerous (e.g. self-driving cars). How do we balance exploration vs safety?
The standard approach is to do the dangerous exploration in simulation first — training the agent through millions of trial-and-error cycles in a safe virtual environment — and only deploy a heavily constrained version to the real world, often with a rule-based safety layer that can override the learned policy in high-risk situations. Real-world "exploration" then becomes narrow and supervised (e.g. controlled test tracks with a safety driver) rather than open-ended trial and error.
Data lakes promise to store "everything," but without governance they become "data swamps." What's the minimum governance needed for a functional data lake?
At minimum: a data catalog documenting what exists and who owns each dataset, consistent naming and partitioning conventions so data can actually be found, and access control tied to the principle of least privilege. Without at least the catalog and ownership piece, a lake accumulates data nobody can locate, trust, or safely reuse — which is the defining symptom of a swamp.
